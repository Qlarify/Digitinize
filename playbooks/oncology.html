The six films this department runs on

Each earns its place. Cut to size, never inflate — a department publishing four of these steadily beats one publishing all six badly.

  • Screening-schedule explainer — Who needs which test at which age, in one calm minute. Screening videos fill camps and catch cancers early — the outcome story every oncology department wants on record.
  • Chemotherapy myths vs facts — A medical oncologist, unhurried, dismantling the three myths every family arrives with: hair, infection risk, "it's a last resort".
  • Day-care chemotherapy suite tour — The recliners, the nurses, the tea, the four-hour rhythm. Patients who have seen the suite arrive less frightened and miss fewer cycles.
  • Patient journey film — Biopsy fear to treatment to the one-year follow-up. Consented survivor stories are the most-watched videos on any oncology page.
  • Breast-lump clinic walk-through — From self-examination to FNAC in one film. Screening numbers move most when the route is familiar before the appointment.
  • Multidisciplinary tumour-board explainer — "Your case is discussed by a team, not one doctor" — trust made visible in ninety seconds.

Consent and dignity in Oncology video

Oncology consent is the strictest in the house: signed before the shoot, the patient shown the cut before publication, diagnosis details at their comfort level, first names only as the default. More families agree than you expect when their own consultant asks after a good outcome — one survivor story a quarter is enough. Any clinical claim passes the medical director's review: no cure language, no survival-rate promises, no comparisons.

Start with the paperwork: our patient video consent kit is free and printable — signed before any recording, filed with the project record.

Measuring it honestly

Track screening-camp registrations against the screening video's publication weeks, day-care completion rates after the suite tour goes live, and the survivor story's completion rate. The metric that matters to the programme is earlier stage at diagnosis — say it honestly when the data is still thin.
