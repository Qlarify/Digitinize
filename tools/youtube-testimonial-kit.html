Hospital YouTube channel checklist & testimonial kit

Two documents hospital marketing teams keep pinned: a scored 30-point audit of your hospital's YouTube presence, and the testimonial kit — the questions to ask a consented patient, and the shots that carry their story with dignity.

Document 1

The 30-point YouTube audit

Tick what is true today. The score updates as you tick — and shows what to fix first.

Channel foundation

  • Channel name and handle match the hospital's brand, not a department's abbreviationPatients search the hospital's name — the channel must answer to it.
  • Banner image states the city, the specialties and one promiseSix words or fewer. A banner is a billboard, not a brochure.
  • Channel description carries the address, phone and OPD hoursIt is indexed. Patients read it before they trust it.
  • A trailer welcomes a first-time visitor within 60 secondsThe one video every new viewer sees. Most hospital channels waste it.
  • Playlists are organised by specialty, not by month"Cardiology" beats "October uploads" in every respect.
  • Links go to the booking page, not the homepageOne fewer click between interest and an OPD slot.

Publishing rhythm

  • A new video goes out at least twice a monthYouTube rewards rhythm more than bursts.
  • Publish day and hour are fixed and known to the teamConsistency compounds; surprise uploads don't.
  • Each video has one doctor and one topic — no marathonsWatch time dies at minute four of a multi-topic recording.
  • Every long video yields at least two vertical cuts for shorts and reelsThe same footage should feed every channel you run.
  • An upload calendar exists on paper, a month aheadIf it isn't scheduled, it isn't a rhythm.

Titles, thumbnails and search

  • Titles are written as the question patients google"When does chest pain need a doctor?" — not "Cardiology CME 2026".
  • Thumbnails show a doctor's face or a patient moment, with three words maximumThe face earns the click; text clutter kills it.
  • Descriptions carry the first 100 characters as the answer itselfThat line shows in search results. Make it useful, not "Welcome to our channel".
  • Every video has chapters with plain-language labelsPatients skip to the minute they need; chapters keep them.
  • The hospital's city appears in titles or tags where it mattersHospital video is local search. "Bengaluru" belongs in the title for OPD-driven topics.

Watch experience

  • Sound is clean — lapel mics, no corridor echoPatients forgive a plain room; they do not forgive noise.
  • The first 10 seconds answer the title's question or promise it tightlyRetention is decided before the doctor finishes their greeting.
  • Every video is subtitled in EnglishMost hospital video is watched muted at first.
  • Regional-language subtitles or versions exist for the top-performing videosThe patients who need you most often watch in their own language.
  • Names and titles on screen are verified with the doctor's officeA mislabelled consultant is a relationship burned for a thumbnail.

Consent and compliance

  • No patient face appears anywhere without a signed consent on fileNot "verbal approval on the day". Signed, filed, retrievable.
  • Consent forms are re-checked before any video is re-used in a campaignPast consent covered past use; check before re-publishing.
  • No cure claims, no survival-rate promises, no competitor comparisons in any title or descriptionCompliance is cheaper than an apology.
  • Clinical scripts and on-screen claims pass a medical reviewOne named reviewer, one day turnaround, no exceptions.

Growth and measurement

  • Videos end with one action: a phone number, a booking link, or a playlist"Subscribe" is not the action a hospital needs.
  • A trackable phone number or link appears in the videos that matterViews are vanity; traced enquiries are the business.
  • Someone reads the retention graph of every uploadThe drop-off minute tells you exactly what to fix next time.
  • Comments get a reply within two working daysThe comment section is a waiting room; staff it like one.
  • The channel is linked from the hospital website, WhatsApp and Google profileYour own properties are the first subscribers and the first trust.

Tick what is true — the bar updates as you go.

Scoring: 24–30 means the machine runs; 15–23 means the parts exist but the habits don't; below 15, start with the foundation section and the publishing rhythm — nothing else pays off until those are fixed.

Document 2

Patient testimonial kit

The questions, the consent flow and the shots. The story belongs to the patient; the recording belongs to the consent form.

Before you ask a patient

  1. Consent first, always. Use the patient video consent form in our consent kit — signed before the shoot, with the patient told exactly where the film appears.
  2. Let the doctor make the ask. Patients agree most readily when their own consultant raises it after a good outcome. A marketer cold-calling the ward does not work.
  3. Choose story over praise. "The staff was excellent" is an advertisement. "I climbed the temple steps I could not climb last year" is a film.
  4. Offer the no-face option. Voice, hands, silhouette — many of the best testimonial films carry no face at all, and consent rates double when patients know that.

Ten questions that make a story

Ask in this order. Follow-ups are where the film lives — the numbered questions only open doors.

  1. "What could you not do before you came to us?"
  2. "What did you think was going to happen?"
  3. "Who was with you when you decided to come here?"
  4. "What do you remember about the first day?"
  5. "What surprised you?"
  6. "Was there a moment you knew you were getting better?"
  7. "What did the doctors and nurses do that stayed with you?"
  8. "What would you say to someone sitting where you sat three months ago?"
  9. "What are you doing now that you couldn't do then?"
  10. "Is there anything you want to add that I didn't ask?"

The interview rules

  • The patient may stop at any time, and is told so before the camera rolls.
  • Never ask for praise, never feed lines, never ask them to repeat something "more positively".
  • Let the answer run. Silence after an answer is where the real answer lives.
  • One clinical sentence from the doctor, if the patient agrees — the story is the patient's, the context is the doctor's.
  • Show the patient the cut before it is published, if they ask to see it.

Shot list for the day

  1. Patient seated at home or workplace, soft window light, camera at eye level — never in a ward gown.
  2. The "before" detail: the stairway, the kitchen, the walking stick — whatever the story named.
  3. Hands and daily actions: tea poured, a grandchild held, a page turned. Dignity lives in small motions.
  4. The doctor segment: consultation room, one or two clinical sentences, warm framing.
  5. The "after" motion: the same action as the "before" detail, now done with ease — film it exactly twice.
  6. Vertical takes of the strongest single answer, for reels and shorts.
  7. Cutaways of the consented family member listening — reaction shots are half the film.
  8. End plate: department, booking number, "shared with the patient's permission".

One rule that outranks the rest: if the patient hesitates — before, during or after — the film waits. No shot is worth a patient who felt used. A story told freely is worth ten arranged ones.
