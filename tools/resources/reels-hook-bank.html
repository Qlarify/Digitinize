30 hooks that stop the scroll

A reel is decided in its first two seconds. These thirty opening lines are sorted by the mechanism that earns attention — urgency, curiosity, myth, story, number, question — each with a filled example and the rule behind it.

Urgency hooks — the ones that bring emergencies in faster

Use sparingly, never fabricate. One urgency hook per department per month is plenty.

  • "Three things your body does before a heart attack that everyone ignores." — Cardiology
  • "If someone collapses in front of you, you have four minutes. Here is what to do." — Emergency
  • "The two-minute check that catches most strokes before they happen." — Neurology
  • "Why waiting out chest pain at home is the most dangerous decision in medicine." — Cardiology
  • "Your child's fever: the one temperature that means go now." — Paediatrics

Curiosity hooks — the ones that earn the full watch

  • "The question I am asked every single day in my OPD — and the answer nobody expects." — any specialty
  • "There is one thing patients always bring to the appointment that they should leave at home." — Gastroenterology
  • "Why your knee pain is worse coming down stairs than going up — and what that tells me." — Orthopaedics
  • "The vitamin that half my patients are deficient in without knowing it." — General medicine
  • "The machine in our lab that patients fear most — and why it is the gentlest one we have." — Endoscopy suite

Myth hooks — the ones that get shared in family groups

  • "Chemo means losing all your hair — no. Here is what actually happens." — Oncology
  • "Diabetes is caused by eating sweets — no, and the real answer is more interesting." — Endocrinology
  • "IVF babies are weaker — no. Let me show you the data I show worried couples." — IVF
  • "You should rest a bad back until it heals — actually the opposite." — Orthopaedics
  • "Antibiotics for every fever — the habit that is quietly making infections stronger." — General

Story hooks — the ones that convert best

Every story hook requires a signed consent before the shoot.

  • "Last year she could not climb these steps. Watch her now." — any recovery story
  • "He postponed his screening for four years. Here is what week one of treatment looked like." — Oncology
  • "The parents who drove six hours for a surgery that took forty minutes." — Paediatric cardiac
  • "She was told she would never carry a child. This is her daughter." — IVF
  • "After the transplant, the first thing he asked for was his bicycle." — Nephrology

Number hooks and question hooks — the reliable workhorses

  • "Five signs your joint pain needs a specialist, not a painkiller." — Orthopaedics
  • "Two minutes, once a month: the self-check every woman should know." — Breast clinic
  • "The ₹0 test that predicts most heart attacks — and it is not what you think." — Cardiology
  • "When should you actually worry about a headache? A neurologist answers." — Neurology
  • "What does a heart-healthy plate look like in an Indian kitchen?" — Cardiology / dietetics

The rules behind every hook that works

  • Two seconds, one claim. The hook is a promise the reel must keep by its last second. A hook that over-promises burns trust you cannot buy back.
  • Say it, do not write it. Spoken hooks with captions beat on-screen text alone; the face carries authority the text cannot.
  • Never invent urgency. Real emergencies justify urgency hooks. Inflated ones get flagged by the same families you want to reach.
  • Match the hook to the doctor. A hook that works for a warm paediatrician dies in the mouth of a hurried consultant. Cast the doctor to the hook, not the hook to the schedule.
  • Bank them. Shoot three hooks per recording session; publish one a week. A hook bank of thirty is a quarter of reels, done in two shoot days.
