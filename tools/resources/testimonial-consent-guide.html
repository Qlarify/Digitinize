Resources for hospital marketing teams

Consent that protects the patient, the doctor and you

A testimonial without proper consent is a liability wearing a smile. This guide walks the whole flow: who asks the patient, what the form says, what happens when a story is re-used, and what to do when someone changes their mind.

Who asks, and when

  • The treating consultant makes the ask, not the marketing desk. After a good outcome, in their own words: "Our team makes short films about patient journeys — would you be open to hearing about it?" Patients refuse marketers and accept their doctors.
  • Ask after recovery has begun, not on admission day. A consent signed in fear or pain is not a consent you want on file.
  • Give 24 hours wherever possible. "Think about it, I will ask again tomorrow" doubles the yes-rate and halves the regret-rate.
  • For minors, the guardian signs — and the child's own willingness is asked too, in words the child understands.

What the form must contain

Our ready-to-print consent form covers all of this — see the consent kit in the free tools. The guide explains each piece so you can defend the policy, not just the paper.

  1. Granular permissions. Face, voice, story, and each channel (website, social media, in-hospital) ticked separately — a patient may say yes to their story but no to their face.
  2. What the video is about. Named plainly: "a film about your heart treatment and recovery". Not "hospital promotional activities".
  3. The no-consequence clause. Refusal changes nothing about their care. Said on the form, said out loud.
  4. The withdrawal right. They may change their mind later, including after publication, and how the takedown works (one working day).
  5. What they are not giving. Medical records access, payment, endorsement of any product — all explicitly out.
  6. Signature lines for the patient (or guardian), a hospital representative, and a witness if your policy wants one.

Re-use, re-publication and the 60-day rule

  • Consent covered the campaign you described. Re-using a story in a new campaign — a paid ad, a different specialty's page — needs a re-check. A phone call, logged with date and outcome, is enough.
  • Re-confirm before any use more than 60 days after signing. Circumstances change; patients move, families change their minds.
  • Anonymised does not mean consent-free. A silhouette with a recognisable voice, a first name plus a named disease, even a "story from our OPD" told in the patient's own words — treat all of it as the patient's material.
  • File everything with the project record. The signed form, the re-confirmation call notes, the takedown log. If it is not filed, it did not happen.

When a patient withdraws

  1. Thank them, immediately. The patient has just done you a favour by telling you instead of telling the internet.
  2. Log it: date, requester, person informed, channels involved.
  3. Take everything down within one working day — the video, the cuts, the reels made from it, the paid placements.
  4. Confirm back to the patient, in writing. The loop closed in writing is the loop that stays closed.
  5. Keep the relationship. A withdrawal handled well often becomes a re-consent a year later; one handled badly becomes a complaint.

The one-line policy worth printing: a story told freely is worth ten arranged ones — and no shot is ever worth a patient who felt used.
