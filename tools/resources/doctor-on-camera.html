Doctor on camera, without the awkwardness

Most consultants fear the camera because the first recording is usually terrible — badly framed, rushed, full of jargon. The fix is preparation, not personality. This is the page to send a doctor the day before their first shoot.

The day before

  • One topic per take, one page of notes. A 60–90 second answer holds about 150–200 spoken words. If your notes need two pages, that is two videos.
  • Write the first sentence out, word for word. Only the first. Once the sentence launches, the rest of you takes over.
  • Rehearse out loud once, standing. The ear catches what the eye misses. Do not rehearse in your head — it hides the stumbles the camera will find.
  • Sleep beats prep. A tired doctor on camera reads as a tired doctor. Do not schedule recordings after a night shift — ever.

What to wear

  • Solids over patterns; mid-tones over stark white (white coats flare on camera — the crew handles it, but a plain shirt underneath helps).
  • No tight stripes or small checks; they shimmer on video.
  • Glasses are fine; the crew angles the light so they do not glare.
  • Nothing that dates the video unnecessarily: hospital branding ages better than conference badges from last year.

On the day

  • Sit back, both feet down. Leaning back a few degrees reads as authority; perching on the chair edge reads as nerves.
  • Talk to the person, not the lens. The crew will stand a friendly face just beside the camera. Speak to them like a patient in your OPD who asked a good question.
  • Slow down 20 per cent. Everyone speeds up on camera. What feels unnaturally slow to you sounds calm and clear on screen.
  • Hands are welcome. A doctor who gestures while explaining is a doctor who looks like themselves. Keep them above the desk, out of pockets.
  • Stumble? Stay in the frame and start the sentence again. The edit removes the stumble. What it cannot remove is the flinch after it.
  • Water, always. A dry mouth clicks on a lapel mic in a way you will not notice and every viewer will.

The five habits of doctors who look natural

  1. They answer a real question, not a syllabus. "When does chest pain need a doctor?" — not "The epidemiology of thoracic discomfort".
  2. They use the words their patients use, once each. Name the thing the way a patient would google it, then the medical term once, then move on.
  3. They say "I don't know" when they don't. The single most trust-building sentence on camera. Follow it with what you do know.
  4. They end with one action. Every good byte closes with what the viewer should do next — book a check, watch a sign, bring a parent.
  5. They let the crew work. Microphone, light, framing, retakes — that is the production's job. The doctor's only job is the answer.

For the marketing team: send this page the day before, book 30 minutes per doctor, and promise two working days to the first cut. The promise you keep on the first video is the reason the second one happens.
