Resources for hospital marketing teams

The video an international patient watches at 2 a.m., deciding

International patients choose a hospital long before they book a flight, from a time zone away, in a second or third language. Video is how they walk your corridors before they ever enter them. This is what to make for them.

What they search, and what answers it

They askThe video that answers it
"Is this hospital real, and is it good?"A calm facility tour — international desk, wards, the suite their treatment uses. No stock footage, no drone montages.
"Who will treat me?"Doctor introductions in their own words, credentials on screen, one clinical sentence each. Not award reels.
"What happens when I arrive?"The arrival walk: airport pickup, registration, the interpreter's desk, the first consultation. The unknown is the fear; the route is the cure.
"Can they communicate with me?"Staff greetings in the languages your international desk actually serves. Thirty seconds, huge reassurance.
"What about after I fly home?"The follow-up story: teleconsultation, reports, the doctor who still answers. Aftercare video is the most under-made international asset there is.

Language strategy

  • Subtitle before you dub. English audio with subtitles in the patient's language covers most needs at a fraction of the cost.
  • Dub the three videos that carry the decision: the facility tour, the doctor introduction, and the arrival walk. Native-speaker voices only — an accented script loses the trust you paid for.
  • Medical terms checked per language, every time. A mistranslated dosage instruction in a subtitle is a patient-safety event, not a typo.
  • Match languages to your actual patient mix — the international desk knows the top five nationalities. Make for who comes, not for who might.

What builds trust across borders (and what breaks it)

  • Process transparency over promises. "Here is what your first week looks like" beats "world-class care" in every market on earth.
  • Real patients, real accents, consented. One international patient telling their story in their own language outperforms every statistic.
  • No price claims in video. Costs differ per case and per nationality's package; pricing belongs in the one-to-one conversation, not on a public timeline.
  • Slow down the edit. International viewers rewatch with subtitles; a frantic pace punishes exactly the viewer you made it for.
  • The visa-and-airport questions answered plainly. Medical-value travel runs on logistics anxiety; a 60-second "how the visa letter and pickup work" video is a service, not marketing.
